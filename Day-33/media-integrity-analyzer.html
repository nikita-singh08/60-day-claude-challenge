Why headlines lie a little (or a lot)

Headlines are written to get clicks, not just to inform. Writers often stretch a small detail into something dramatic because dramatic headlines travel faster. This gap between the headline's claim and the article's actual content is called headline–content mismatch.

Everyday example: a headline says "Study PROVES coffee cures cancer" — but the actual study only found a small correlation in mice. The excitement is manufactured.

    Emotion Detector

    💓

    Content is often designed to make you feel first, think later

    Posts that trigger strong emotions — fear, outrage, awe, envy — spread faster because emotional content is more shareable. Recognizing when you're being emotionally steered is the first step to reacting with your head instead of just your gut.

    Everyday example: a caption reading "You won't BELIEVE what your landlord doesn't want you to know" is built to trigger outrage and curiosity before you've read a single fact.
